Securing the Connected Patient: Vulnerabilities in Networked Medical Devices and a Clinical-Impact Framework for Prioritizing Them

Dwij Bhatt · John P. Stevens High School, Edison, NJ · [ADD date / mentor]

← Back to projects

Abstract

Pacemakers, insulin pumps, infusion pumps, and imaging systems now run software and connect to wireless or hospital networks. That connectivity improves care, but it also means a software flaw can put a patient's health at risk. This paper reviews major publicly disclosed medical device vulnerabilities from 2011 onward and summarizes how U.S. regulation has responded, especially Section 524B of the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act, in effect since 2023. It then argues that standard vulnerability scores, which measure technical severity, do not capture clinical harm. As an alternative, I propose a Clinical-Impact-Weighted Risk Score (CIWRS) that combines exploitability, potential patient harm, and device exposure. The next phase of this project applies the score to a dataset of public CISA medical advisories to test whether it ranks vulnerabilities differently than technical scores alone.

1. Introduction

A modern hospital can have more connected devices than staff. Bedside monitors report to central stations, infusion pumps receive drug libraries over Wi-Fi, and implanted cardiac devices send data to physicians from patients' homes. Each connection is useful, and each one is also a possible entry point for an attacker.

In most computer systems, a successful attack means lost data or money. In medical devices, the consequence can be physical: a wrong drug dose, a disabled alarm, or a device that stops working. This project asks two questions:

  • RQ1: What kinds of vulnerabilities have been publicly disclosed in connected medical devices, and how have manufacturers and regulators responded?
  • RQ2: Would ranking vulnerabilities by potential clinical impact, rather than by technical severity alone, change which ones get fixed first?

2. What makes medical devices different

Securing a medical device is harder than securing a laptop, for several reasons:

  • Long lifespans. Hospital equipment is often used for many years, and an implanted device stays in a patient for its whole battery life. Software written for one threat environment ends up running in another.
  • Limited resources. Implants run on small batteries and simple processors. Strong encryption and frequent updates use power and computing capacity the device may not have.
  • Hard-to-apply patches. Updating a laptop takes minutes. Updating a pacemaker may require a clinic visit, and changes to device software can require regulatory review.
  • Safety over security. Devices are designed to fail "open" so clinicians can always reach them in an emergency. Security controls like passwords can conflict with that goal.
  • Shared components. Many devices reuse the same operating systems and wireless chips, so one flaw can affect products from many manufacturers at once.

Pattern: The same themes keep appearing. Wireless protocols lacked authentication, legacy devices could not be updated, and third-party components carried flaws into many products. The 2019 insulin pump recall shows that the fix is sometimes to replace the device entirely.

4. The regulatory response

U.S. regulation has moved from voluntary guidance toward legal requirements:

  • 2014: FDA premarket guidance asked manufacturers to address cybersecurity in device submissions.
  • 2016: FDA postmarket guidance described how manufacturers should monitor and respond to vulnerabilities after a device is on the market.
  • 2023: The Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2023 added Section 524B to the Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act. Since March 2023, manufacturers of "cyber devices" must submit a plan to monitor and address vulnerabilities, provide a Software Bill of Materials (SBOM) listing the software components inside the device, and show that the device can be updated and patched.

The SBOM requirement directly addresses the supply-chain problem from URGENT/11 and SweynTooth. When a flaw is found in a shared component, hospitals and regulators can quickly identify which devices contain it. The law mainly applies to new devices, though, and many older devices remain in use.

5. The gap: technical severity ≠ patient harm

Security teams usually prioritize vulnerabilities using the Common Vulnerability Scoring System (CVSS), a 0–10 score based mostly on how easy a flaw is to exploit and what it does to confidentiality, integrity, and availability. CVSS was designed for IT systems, so it doesn't consider what the device does to a body.

Consider two hypothetical flaws:

  • Flaw A: A remotely exploitable bug that leaks non-sensitive logs from a hospital thermometer station. The CVSS score might be high because it is easy to exploit over the network.
  • Flaw B: A bug that requires an attacker to be physically close, but that could change the dose on an insulin pump. The CVSS score might be moderate because proximity makes it harder to exploit.

From a patient's point of view, Flaw B is clearly more dangerous. A hospital with limited staff that fixes vulnerabilities in CVSS order might address Flaw A first. MITRE has published a rubric for applying CVSS to medical devices, which shows the field recognizes this problem. This project explores a simpler score that makes clinical impact explicit.

6. Proposed framework: Clinical-Impact-Weighted Risk Score (CIWRS)

CIWRS multiplies three factors, each scored from 1 to 5:

CIWRS = Exploitability × Clinical Harm × Exposure   (range 1–125)
  • Exploitability (E): How easy is the attack? 1 = physical access plus special equipment. 5 = remote, over the internet, no authentication.
  • Clinical Harm (H): The worst realistic outcome for a patient. 1 = no patient effect (e.g., a data leak only). 3 = delayed or degraded care. 5 = direct life-threatening effect (e.g., a therapy change in an implant or pump).
  • Exposure (X): How many patients, and how long? 1 = rare device, easy to patch. 5 = widely deployed, cannot be patched, or implanted.

Multiplying the factors means a vulnerability scores highly only when it is both reachable and dangerous. The example calculator below shows how the two flaws from Section 5 compare.

7. Methodology for the next phase

To test RQ2, the next phase will:

  1. Collect data: Gather the Industrial Control Systems Medical Advisories (ICSMA) published by CISA, which describe vulnerabilities in medical products along with their CVSS scores.
  2. Code each advisory: Record device category, attack vector, vulnerability type, CVSS score, patch availability, and whether the device is implanted.
  3. Assign CIWRS scores with a written rubric, and have a second reviewer [ADD: mentor / clinician] score a sample to check consistency.
  4. Compare rankings: Measure how differently CVSS and CIWRS order the same vulnerabilities (for example, with a rank correlation), and identify the cases where they disagree most.
  5. Report trends by device category and year.

8. Recommendations

  • Hospitals: Keep an inventory of connected devices, place medical devices on separate network segments, and use SBOMs to respond quickly when component flaws are disclosed.
  • Manufacturers: Build authentication into wireless protocols from the start and design devices so they can be updated securely over their whole life.
  • Regulators and researchers: Include clinical impact in how vulnerabilities are prioritized, and address the large number of older devices that current rules don't cover.
  • Patients: Known device vulnerabilities have rarely, if ever, been confirmed as used to harm a patient. The benefits of these devices far outweigh the risk, and patients should follow their doctor's advice about updates.

9. Limitations

This study uses only publicly disclosed vulnerabilities, which likely undercounts the real total. CIWRS scoring involves judgment, especially for clinical harm, so input from clinicians is important. The framework is a proposal meant to show a gap in current prioritization, not a validated clinical tool.
